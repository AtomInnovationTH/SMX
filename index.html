You reached the Kármán Line (100 km), but that's only the start.
A real space-elevator seed tether continues to geostationary orbit (35,786 km), where gravity and centrifugal force balance, and on to a counterweight beyond: above GEO the effective gravity flips outward. This game stops at Kármán; the rest is the concept it points to.

You flew the up-climber: carry cargo up, ride the beamed waves, manage EPM charge. You also met two descenders on the way, and their energy went into the ribbon as vibration (Lofstrom). Powering climbers from the top, with space solar or with descending climbers, is one of the paper's own open ideas (p.14), and flying one is not in this game yet.

Settings (Press S to toggle)

Your climber's propulsion is a stack of electro-permanent magnets (EPMs): momentum crosses an air gap by pulsed eddy-current traction in the conductive graphene tether. The tether runs through the gap between two opposing magnet stacks, and it is moving up and down. hold SPACE to engage the stacks and induce eddy currents in the moving tether. The onboard controller flicks the magnets on while the tether is moving up, and the eddy currents transfer momentum to your climber. It steers the climber with slight timing adjustments to the left and right magnets of each stack, like tires on a car, and it sends current to the opposing magnets to center itself on the tether.

Nothing ever touches the tether. No wheel, no clamp, no friction to wear out, no damage to the tether.

Why an EPM? An electromagnet is too heavy and burns power continuously just to hold a field. A permanent magnet can never switch off. An EPM is the only device that is on for free and off for free: it costs only a few joules per switch. Zero static power is the enabling technology; without it the climber would spend more energy holding on than climbing.

Coupling hardware modelled on Zubax FluxGrip FG40 electro-permanent magnets; no affiliation or endorsement implied. FG40 works in the lab on 1 mm aluminum: it does transfer momentum, and its slow switching on/off requires long waves.
Presets: Entry points, all applied through the sliders below. Lofstrom 1000 Hz is the wall demo: at 1000 Hz the same 8-pair stack draws 128 kW of switching, which empties the climber's own 0.19 MJ buffer in about a second and a half: it browns out no matter how much thrust it has. That wall is Gassend's p.11 story. Wessels keeps his 92 Hz / 60 cm / 350 m/s ribbon speed; his 40 m wavelength implies a slower tether than this film (c = 3.7 vs 20.9 km/s), so λ comes out 227 m here.

Ground station

How fast the ground station drives the wave, 92-1000 Hz (Wessels to Lofstrom). Carrier sets the wavelength (λ = c/f) and the stroke cap, and the SWITCHING cost: with this film class the energy loop stalls above ~200 Hz. Find the wall; that's the paper's story.
Ground stroke per cycle, in real metres. The stress budget caps it (A·ω·√R ≤ v_max, where R is the taper below: wave stress peaks at the film's thinnest section, so that is where the cap binds). Raise the carrier and the cap drops; the label then reads "(capped)" and shows the stroke actually in use. The film's drawn wiggle is enlarged ~8x and slowed so you can SEE your stroke; the physics runs the real metres at the real frequency.
Fraction of the film's working stress the wave may use: v_max = budget × strength ÷ √(Eρ). The paper's "10% of tether strength at 1000 km/h" is a share of stress-limited POWER (P ∝ σ²): at 30% stress you use 9% of it, which is why both numbers are shown.
Wave now: …
Where thrust balance settles on the pad: the wave's push fades as you close on the film speed, and cruise is where what's left equals your weight. The film runs a few per cent slower aloft (the p.7 drag tax), so the real cruise sits just under this; and the push is only half the story - the switching bill above has to be earned back too, which is the wall the Lofstrom preset demonstrates.
4·N·E_switch·f: flat in duty cycle, two transitions per cycle per FG40 unit. This is the skim the wave must earn back before your magnets brown out; the paper's whole delivery budget is 4 MW (p.2).
This is the power the wave carries past you: slide 6's transported power, P = vF = Z·V² = ρ·c·A·V². The impedance Z = ρ·c·A is 48 N/(m/s)/mm² for this film class, exactly the table's longitudinal row. The figure is computed on the local film at your altitude, with the same formula your stack's coupling draws against, so it can never disagree with the physics. Two things move it. Taper (p.9) holds transported power constant as the section thins, while the p.7 drag tax saps a few percent over the column, so the figure drifts down a little as you climb. Under resonance the scarce thing is the ground station's injection instead (p.10's P = σ·v, the budget the amplitude slider then sets), and the line switches to that. With a second climber sharing (p.14), this is the budget both skims cap against, minus his draw. Read it next to the switching and stack-heat rows: the wave carries MW against the stack's kW. That gap is the paper's whole argument for mechanical power.
The hot side of the energy loop. While you hold, the switching watts above land in the magnets as heat, at the datasheet's own 4 J per transition. The FG40 publishes a ceiling of +73 °C internal, an absolute maximum set by the heat-deflection limit of the polymer body (the electronics is good to 105 °C). Low down, the moving air carries that heat away. As the air thins, the stack is left with radiation only. Could we show a temperature instead? Not without inventing numbers: no source publishes the heat capacity, thermal resistance or emissivity you would need, so the game books the exact watts against the exact ceiling and models no stack temperature. The film warms the same way, in theory. Its drag dissipation (the 2 km beat's bill) is the paper's "possibly by drag heating" (p.7), but the paper does not say where that heat lands, air or film, so no film temperature is modelled either.
The paper's p.10 lever. Engage it and the ground station becomes a node: a standing wave fills the film between ground station and climber, and its tension holds the full stress budget. The film then runs its local stress ceiling whatever your stroke, so power per unit drive speed rises by v_cap/v_drive: the table's 2.5 MW/mm² at 200 km/h, against 150 kW plain. The price is that the cavity only accepts its own modes. Your carrier is overridden by f = n·c/2h, so the frequency falls as you climb, and each time the wavelength reaches the 100 km floor the ground station retunes to the next harmonic and pays one cavity round trip of weakened film (the 50 km transient). Switching follows the cavity rate: nearly free aloft, kHz on the pad. Engage it once you are a kilometre up, not on the grass. While resonant, the amplitude slider becomes the power budget: your skim cannot exceed the ground station's injection (stress budget × drive speed × section, the table's own P = σ·v). The boundary-value buildup is summarised as the one-round-trip ramp, and the reflection interplay the paper calls tricky for multi-climber is not modelled.
The paper's p.14 open question, how to power more than one climber, playable at the budget level only. Refuse (the default) and you climb alone. Share, and when a second climber requests power at 85 km it boards your wave: the wave's transported power becomes a shared budget, and each rider's skim caps at the budget minus the other's draw. What that costs depends on the mode. A plain wave carries P = ρ·c·A·V² (slide 6), about 140 MW here against your ~13 kW skim, so sharing costs nothing you can feel. Under resonance the budget is the ground station's injection instead (the P = σ·v the amplitude slider budgets), so sharing halves your cruise: the paper's "retuning makes sharing tricky" (p.10), felt. The other rider is summarised as a twin in formation cruise (drawing weight × climb speed). What stays absent is the wave physics between riders: partial reflections (p.3) and the standing-pattern perturbation (p.10) are not modelled.
The paper's p.12 table sorts ribbon waves into longitudinal vs transverse, each travelling or standing. You fly the longitudinal travelling cell; the resonance lever above moves you into longitudinal standing and back, the one mode change the paper supports. The transverse cells stay absent: a transverse wave is dead at 45 km/h in air (p.7's drag row), and above the atmosphere the paper only asks "consider mode conversion?" (p.12) and warns that every method on its p.13 ladder has substantial unsolved challenges. No converter exists in the paper, so none is modelled.
Wave shape (1 / 2 / 3): a symmetric sine needs you to pulse in rhythm (forgiving, wide window). An asymmetric sawtooth behaves like a traveling-wave / ratchet motor: it gives a net upward push even with steady pulsing (efficient). A square wave only couples at its sharp edges (extreme skill).

Tether

Carbon films by working STRENGTH: stiffness (~1 TPa) and density (2300 kg/m³) barely vary across them, so strength alone sets the wave's speed ceiling (v_max, below). Entries above ~130 GPa exceed anything the source paper assumes and are marked speculative.
The tether is a paper-thin conductive FILM, not a cable: on the real elevator a ribbon about 30 cm wide and under 0.1 mm thick, silverish like multilayer graphene or aluminum foil (the paper's "typical elevator ribbon is 20 mm²"). This game flies the ~10 mm² class the paper's wave tables assume: 45 mm × 0.2 mm = 9 mm²; the slider spans both. The drawn band follows this slider up to about 50 mm and then stops widening, because past that it would grow into the climber's clamps and the air gap would stop being visible, which is the one thing the picture must not say. The number here is the width the physics uses.
Thicker film couples harder per pair (k ∝ t) but adds mass per metre. The real tether runs at under 0.1 mm; the default 0.2 mm is the paper's worked example, and the slider floor (0.05 mm) reaches the real range.
width × thickness, the conductor the eddy currents flow in. With taper this is the section ALOFT; the ground station's is R times wider.
The paper's p.9 lever: a wider ribbon at the ground station, thinning with altitude. Transported power is constant, so the wave's speed and amplitude adjust as 1/√A with height, and the ground station runs √R slower for the same speed at the top. R = 4 is the paper's own example: a 500 km/h ground station for 1000 km/h aloft. What you pay is the start. The low ceiling drops by the same factor, and from about R = 3 the low-climb skim can no longer cover the flat switching draw. R = 2.5 flies clean; the demo stack browns out before break-even at R = 3, and a bigger stack does not help, because the break-even is per pair. A trade, not a free lunch. The stress cap binds at the thin top, tightening the stroke cap by 1/√R. Taper is in the ribbon width; the thickness the coupling reads stays uniform, and the drawn band tracks the local width up to the same clamp-jaw cap as before. The paper's own cost is tether mass (2.3 t for its 5.4 MW example), which is the ground station's problem and is not modelled. The taper is slow against the wavelength (100 km against 227 m), so the paper promises no reflections; its λ/4 anti-reflection layer is the other route, also not modelled.
Pretension keeps the film taut and flat as it runs through the gap between the opposed magnet pairs. A slack film flutters, and flutter eats the clearance the coupling needs.
Longitudinal (compression) wave speed, set by the material's stiffness and density: this is what your pulses couple to.

Climber

Opposed pairs in the climber's FG40 sandwich, the REAL scaling axis (not a magnet-material ladder: Alnico + NdFeB co-exist in every unit). More pairs: more thrust, but more stack mass and more switching watts. The demo default is 8 pairs (16 units, 33 cm, 1.4 kg) carrying 3 kg at about 3:1 thrust-to-weight; the same ratio at Gassend's §2.5 anchor is ~64 pairs for 25 kg, and 64 is the top of this slider.
The working gap between the opposed FG40 pairs and the film: nothing ever touches. Tighter couples MUCH harder (FG40's published force-vs-airgap curve: 100% at 0.1 mm, ~16% at 0.5 mm) but leaves less centering margin for film flutter. M1.5's field-strength multiplier was an interim label; the gap is the real control.
The onboard controller holds the film centred between the opposed poles: automatic, not your job. What you own is the MARGIN: gap per side minus film flutter (±0.1 mm at 100 kgf pretension, shrinking as √tension). Margin ≤ 0 and the controller unloads the stack rather than scrape the film; the stack label says so on canvas too.
2 FG40 units per opposed pair, 72 g each, plus 25% structure. Dry mass is dead weight you carry; cargo (below) is what scores.
Scored payload, kg. More cargo: more inertia (a = F/m), but cargo delivered to 100 km is your score. The top of this slider is roughly what a full 64-pair stack lifts at the same thrust-to-weight the 8-pair demo flies.
1.00 G = 9.81 m/s². Pulls the climber down; you beat it only by coupling to the wave.
How much air is left at your altitude (US Standard Atmosphere): the factor that scales the wave's aerodynamic drag (paper p.7). The climber feels the air only as a damped film, never directly.

Accessibility

Still Unsolved 🤔

This is live research, not settled engineering. The playable climb stops at 100 km; the real seed tether continues to geostationary orbit, 358× further. But the harder questions are not about distance: they are the ones the paper this game illustrates ends on, still open, and asked of its audience:
• What drag coefficient does a longitudinal wave actually have? The paper's 0.02 is labelled a guess, and the entire in-atmosphere power limit hangs off it.
• What else could limit in-atmosphere speed?
• Is drag heating real? The paper's p.7 says the longitudinal wave will be limited by stress, wave-generator strength-to-weight and "possibly by drag heating", and stops there: no temperature, no rate. The wave's drag bill is live in this game (the 2 km beat quotes it), but where that heat lands and what it raises is not published, so no film temperature is modelled. Your stack's heat is booked the same way: the datasheet's 4 J per transition and +73 °C internal ceiling are exact, the thermal resistance between them is not published, so no stack temperature is modelled either.
• How unreasonable is 1000 km/h machinery, really?
• What are the ways for a climber to extract the power? This game commits to one answer, pulsed eddy-current traction across an air gap, but that is a guess of ours, not a finding of the paper's.
• How do you power more than one climber? The budget answer is playable now: share the wave above 85 km (Settings, Ground station) and each rider's skim caps at the budget minus the other's draw. Still unsolved and NOT modelled: the wave physics between riders (partial reflections, p.3; the retune interplay that makes sharing tricky, p.10).
• Can a wave change mode, longitudinal through the atmosphere and transverse above it? The paper's p.12 table ends on exactly that question and p.14 repeats the maybe, but no converter, coupling length or efficiency is offered anywhere, so none is modelled. The transverse cells stay captions (dead at 45 km/h in air, p.7); the one mode change that ships is travelling to standing, the resonance lever (p.10).
If you have an answer to any of these, the author is actively asking for one. The paper and where to find it: the Sources & credits button at the top of this panel.
